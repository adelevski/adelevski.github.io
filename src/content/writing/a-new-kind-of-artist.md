Through snowball, I feel as though I am becoming a kind of artist—but perhaps not an artist in the traditional sense.

Software is becoming my medium. Instead of producing paintings, music, or stories, I want to produce useful and beautiful digital public goods: tools shaped by my own opinions, judgment, and taste, then made available for other people to use, modify, and build upon.

These creations are digital because they exist through computers and phones. They are public because I intend to release them under permissive open-source licenses such as Apache 2.0, allowing people to freely use, modify, redistribute, and even commercialize them within the license’s conditions. They are goods because they provide genuine utility. People can use them to solve problems, improve their lives, or pursue purposes I may never have anticipated.

This resembles art more than I initially realized. Traditional artists make subjective choices about what deserves to exist and how it should feel. Their work reflects a particular vision of the world. Not everyone will appreciate the result, but some people may connect with it deeply.

Software can work the same way. A tool does not have to appeal to everyone to be meaningful. It can embody a particular philosophy about simplicity, beauty, privacy, openness, or how computers ought to serve people. Its usefulness does not make it less expressive. Utility can itself be part of the artistic vision.

DHH describes Omarchy as his “omakase” operating system: a deliberately curated experience built from his own preferences about how a computer should look and behave. snowball could become my omakase of digital public goods—a collection of software reflecting what I believe deserves to exist and what I think certain people might genuinely enjoy using.

I am comfortable admitting that DHH’s motivation resonates strongly with me. I also want to build for people who love computers and believe software should be malleable, thoughtful, and beautiful. The goal is not to reproduce his work, but to adopt a similar creative posture: develop a point of view, express it through software, and offer the result to anyone who finds it valuable.

AI makes this possibility especially interesting. As programming and product development become increasingly automated, implementation alone may become less scarce. What becomes more important is deciding what should be built, why it should exist, how it should work, and which compromises are acceptable.

The people who succeed in that environment may be those who can think critically, exercise taste, form a coherent vision, and make decisive choices. They will orchestrate increasingly capable AI systems, but the direction will still come from human judgment. The machine may generate the implementation; the creator remains responsible for the intent.

This does not mean that anyone who prompts an AI system automatically becomes an artist. The creative contribution lies in developing a worthwhile vision and carrying it through countless decisions until the result becomes coherent, distinctive, and useful. Taste must produce outcomes.

I do not yet have DHH’s pedigree or history of successful work. That is not a philosophical problem; it is simply something that must be earned. The only credible path is to keep building, learning, refining my judgment, and releasing things that people find valuable. Over time, the individual projects can become a body of work.

Perhaps “artist” is not the complete description. This role also contains elements of the designer, engineer, curator, craftsperson, and public steward. But artist captures something important: the ambition is not merely to generate software efficiently. It is to channel a personal vision into things that deserve to exist—and then give those things to the world.
