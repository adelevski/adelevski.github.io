I am a programmer and quantitative analyst. Most of my work involves mathematical modeling and Python. Web development and user interfaces were things I hadn't bothered learning and wasn't interested in doing myself. AI has changed that. It helps me use libraries I previously wouldn't have attempted and build things I would have found a pain in the butt to do myself. Now I can describe what I want, sometimes literally with my voice, and help bring those ideas into existence. That's how I've been building snowball and my personal website.

How can I look at this and not love it to its absolute core? I have been fully pilled, as they say.

At work, one maritime project involved hundreds of formulas translated by hand from regulatory Excel workbooks. Their dependencies ran many levels deep, with exceptions introduced for reasons given by subject matter experts. Adding a new ship type meant tracing all of that again. AI helped me inspect the formulas, collect the deviations, and turn the process into a code-generation framework. I delivered a solution in a couple of weeks that I expected would previously have taken months. I could have written the framework myself, but the effort of getting there would have been much greater. It's absolutely nuts.

That changes how I think about learning. Having less to learn before I can start lets me pursue more ideas and learn what those ideas require along the way. I can ask for an explanation, work through a problem, or explore a library in the context of something I actually want to accomplish.

With fairway and modo, I still needed to study the underlying mathematics to reason about how the tools should work and present their results. Learning more about road networks and driving times helped me see two different questions inside what had been one project: where a group could meet, and which actual golf course would serve them best. That helped me separate them into two projects.

modo initially offered two ways to find a meeting area: minimize everyone's combined driving time, or minimize the longest individual drive. Using it and talking with my dad made me favor the second. We cared about the person who would otherwise spend the longest driving, and I chose to simplify the interface around that preference.

Then, while discussing this essay with AI, I reconsidered. Reducing the longest drive can increase the group's combined travel. Both choices have a case, depending on what the group cares about. Understanding that tradeoff made me want both options back.

I'm still learning the math, and I want to spend more time with it. What I have learned has already changed what I'm building. To decide how best to present something, I usually need to understand how it works.

I still enjoy reading, too. Text lets me stop, ponder, and continue at my own pace. I can keep several things in view while reasoning through a complicated system. AI gives me more ways to learn, and I still need time to think about what I'm learning.

I've been using software since my parents put me in front of one of my dad's computers when I was two. At thirty, that's twenty-eight years of experiencing digital goods. Along the way I've formed opinions about how software should look, how it should feel, and which interactions are pleasant. Those opinions keep changing with experience, curiosity, and iteration.

Through snowball, software is becoming my artistic medium. I want the tools to express those opinions through something useful and beautiful that people can use, modify, share, and build on. Utility can be part of the expression. I'm borrowing from DHH when I say that if I make software I find pleasant to use, I expect at least a handful of other people will agree, even if they wouldn't put in the effort to build it themselves. I am building this for me and them.

AI participates in that process by helping me reason, explain, visualize, and reconsider. When I think about the relationship I want with it, I guess it's this kind, except more. The better it helps me understand something, the better I can think about it. I want it to stay helpful to me and my curiosities, understand what I'm trying to accomplish, and offer useful criticism when I might be thinking about something wrong. Intellectual and honest deliberation is part of what makes it valuable.

I can imagine that becoming an increasingly personal kind of mentorship: learning what I need for the things I choose to pursue. I'm optimistic because of what I already experience. I also want privacy, control over my own direction, and a clear view of where the assistance falls short. I remain responsible for the things I decide to release.

That responsibility includes deciding what this new capacity should serve. I'm upset with how much grift and greed there is in tech, and with how bad a name AI has gotten. I appreciate the value of capitalism, but I disagree with the endless pursuit of more. I'm tired of subscriptions, advertising, and incentives to keep extracting value from people. I want to use these capabilities to make more useful things freely available.

The [principles behind snowball](https://snowball-projects.github.io/principles/) describe what matters to me: public benefit, openness, minimal data, no surveillance or advertising, and tools that remain simple to maintain. Maximum value with least complexity. There will still be room for software companies. I also think there is room for much more shared public software whose purpose is to benefit the people using it.

Faster development doesn't make maintenance disappear. But whatever a person or small group can build and sustain without huge investment or an incentive to chase profit should be considered and tried, as long as it provides enough value to warrant the effort. I want snowball to explore that possibility through the work itself. I will keep building, learning, and refining my judgment, and let the things I release show what I mean.
